Part 1 — How well do you know the means?#

Q1 — Means, standard errors, and whether you can tell them apart#

For each industry compute the sample mean excess return, its standard error

\[\operatorname{se}(\bar r_i) = \frac{\sigma_i}{\sqrt{T}}\]

and the 95% confidence interval \(\bar r_i \pm 1.96\, \operatorname{se}(\bar r_i)\). The formula assumes returns are uncorrelated from one month to the next — not a bad assumption for monthly returns. Annualize all of it (times 12) and keep it in one DataFrame, ci, one row per industry, with columns mean, se, lower, upper.

Report how many of the 49 intervals exclude zero, the spread of the 49 means (their standard deviation across industries), and the median standard error.

Can you tell them apart? The tangency portfolio does not care whether industries earn a premium. It cares which ones earn more than the others. So build each industry’s return minus the average of all 49 in the same month, and test whether the mean of that difference is zero, with the same standard-error formula.

Part 2 — The tangency portfolio#

Q2 — The weights#

From Capital Allocation I: the portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio has weights proportional to

\[w \;\propto\; \Sigma^{-1} \mu\]

Use the sample means mu = df.mean() and the sample covariance S = df.cov(), both monthly. Then scale the weights so the portfolio runs 10% annualized volatility: the monthly variance of a portfolio \(w\) is \(w'\Sigma w\), so multiply \(w\) by \(0.10 / \sqrt{12\, w'\Sigma w}\).

You will build this portfolio about 2,000 times before you are done, so it is worth writing once as a function that takes a mean vector and a covariance matrix and returns the scaled weights.

Store the weights in a DataFrame W, one row per industry, in a column called 'sample'. Report the portfolio’s in-sample Sharpe ratio, the Sharpe ratio of 1/N (equal weight in all 49) and of the market, the number of short positions, and the gross exposure \(\sum_i |w_i|\).

Q4 — What it costs#

Compute the Sharpe ratio of each column of W using the sample moments mu and S — as if the sample were the truth. Under that truth 'sample' is the best you can do, and the other two were built with a wrong mean for Hshld:

\[SR(w) = \sqrt{12}\; \frac{w'\mu}{\sqrt{w'\Sigma w}}\]

Now do it for every industry: loop over the 49, move each one’s mean to the lower and to the upper end of its interval (one at a time, the other 48 at their sample values), and record the average percentage loss in Sharpe ratio:

\[\text{loss}_i \;=\; 1 - \frac{\tfrac12\left[\,SR(i\ \text{low}) + SR(i\ \text{high})\,\right]}{SR(\text{sample})}\]

Part 3 — Two worlds where you know the answer#

In real data you never learn the true means, so you can never check how far off you are. A simulation gets around that: declare a truth, draw fake histories from it, and see what someone with 686 months of data would have found.
